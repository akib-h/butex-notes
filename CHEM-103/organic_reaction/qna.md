## 1. Inductive Effect

**Q1.1.** Arrange these groups in decreasing order of −I effect: −Cl, −NO₂, −OH, −COOH.

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

$$-\text{NO}_2 > -\text{COOH} > -\text{Cl} > -\text{OH}$$

−NO₂ has the strongest electron withdrawal due to the highly electronegative, resonance-stabilised nitro group; −OH is the weakest of the four because oxygen's lone pairs partially offset its electronegativity through a small +M contribution.

</details>

---

**Q1.2.** Why does the inductive effect become negligible beyond the third carbon from the substituent?

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

Each successive σ bond only transmits a fraction of the polarisation from the one before it, so the induced charge decays multiplicatively along the chain. By the third carbon the residual displacement is too small to affect reactivity meaningfully — this is why inductive effects are described as short-range, unlike resonance which can act over the whole conjugated system.

</details>

---

**Q1.3.** Explain, using the inductive effect, why trichloroacetic acid (Cl₃CCOOH) is a much stronger acid than acetic acid (CH₃COOH).

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

The three chlorine atoms exert a strong cumulative −I effect, pulling electron density away from the O–H bond and the carboxylate oxygen. This stabilises the conjugate base (trichloroacetate ion) far more than the methyl group stabilises acetate — methyl is a weak +I donor that actually destabilises the negative charge slightly. Greater conjugate-base stability means a much lower pKₐ (trichloroacetic acid ≈ 0.7 vs acetic acid ≈ 4.76).

</details>

---

**Q1.4.** Rank in order of increasing acid strength: formic acid (HCOOH), acetic acid (CH₃COOH), propanoic acid (CH₃CH₂COOH).

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

$$\text{propanoic acid} < \text{acetic acid} < \text{formic acid}$$

Formic acid has no alkyl group at all, so there's no +I donation destabilising the conjugate base. Each additional alkyl carbon (methyl, then ethyl) pushes more electron density onto the carboxylate, making it less stable and the parent acid progressively weaker.

</details>

## 2. Electromeric Effect

**Q2.1.** State two conditions necessary for the electromeric effect to occur.

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

1. A multiple bond (π system — C=C, C=O, C≡C, etc.) must be present.
2. An attacking reagent (electrophile or nucleophile) must approach the π system — the effect is triggered only in the presence of the reagent and disappears the instant it's removed.

</details>

---

**Q2.2.** Distinguish +E and −E effect with one example each.

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

+E effect: the π electron pair shifts toward the atom being attacked by the electrophile. Example: attack of H⁺ on propene, where the π pair moves toward the carbon that ends up bonded to hydrogen, generating the more stable carbocation.

−E effect: the π electron pair shifts away from the attacking species. Example: HCN addition to a carbonyl, where the C=O π pair moves fully onto oxygen as the nucleophile (CN⁻) attacks carbon.

</details>

---

**Q2.3.** Why is the electromeric effect described as temporary while the inductive and mesomeric effects are described as permanent?

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

The electromeric effect exists only during the actual moment of reagent attack — complete transfer of a π electron pair happens transiently to accommodate the incoming electrophile or nucleophile, then the system relaxes. Inductive and mesomeric effects, by contrast, are built into the ground-state electronic structure of the molecule and are present whether or not a reagent is nearby, which is why they show up in measurable ground-state properties like dipole moment and bond length.

</details>

---

**Q2.4.** In the addition of HBr to but-2-ene, which bond's electrons undergo the electromeric shift, and in which direction?

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

The C=C π electrons shift completely onto one of the two alkene carbons as H⁺ approaches, forming a new C–H σ bond and leaving a carbocation on the other carbon. Since both carbons in but-2-ene are equivalent secondary centres, either carbon can end up as the cation — the electromeric shift itself is what generates the intermediate that subsequent Br⁻ attack will quench.

</details>

## 3. Mesomeric Effect

**Q3.1.** Classify −NH₂, −OCH₃, and −NO₂ as +M or −M groups, and explain the electronic basis for each.

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

−NH₂ and −OCH₃ are +M groups: nitrogen and oxygen each carry a lone pair that can delocalise into an adjacent π system, donating electron density.

−NO₂ is a −M group: it has no lone pair to donate into the ring and instead its own π* system withdraws electron density from the attached π system through resonance, spreading negative charge onto the oxygens.

</details>

---

**Q3.2.** Why is aniline (C₆H₅NH₂) a weaker base than methylamine (CH₃NH₂)?

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

In aniline, the nitrogen lone pair is delocalised into the benzene ring through resonance (+M effect), spreading the electron density across the ring rather than concentrating it on nitrogen. A less available lone pair means nitrogen is less able to accept a proton. Methylamine has no such delocalisation pathway — its nitrogen lone pair is fully localised and freely available for protonation, making it the stronger base.

</details>

---

**Q3.3.** Chlorobenzene undergoes electrophilic aromatic substitution more slowly than benzene, yet chlorine still directs incoming electrophiles to the ortho/para positions. Reconcile these two facts using inductive and mesomeric effects.

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

Chlorine has a strong −I effect (electronegative, withdraws through σ bonds) but a weaker +M effect (its lone pair can still donate into the ring, though less efficiently than nitrogen or oxygen due to the size mismatch between chlorine's 3p orbital and carbon's 2p orbital). The −I effect dominates overall, pulling electron density out of the ring and deactivating it toward electrophiles — hence the slower reaction. But where the +M effect does operate, it selectively raises electron density at the ortho and para positions relative to meta, so whatever reaction does occur is directed there.

</details>

---

**Q3.4.** Explain why the −COOH group is both ring-deactivating and meta-directing in electrophilic aromatic substitution.

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

The carbonyl carbon of −COOH is electron-poor and, through resonance, withdraws π electron density from the ring (−M effect), while the group's electronegativity also withdraws through σ bonds (−I effect). Both effects lower electron density everywhere on the ring, but the ortho and para positions lose the most density because those are the positions resonance structures place formal positive charge on directly. This leaves the meta position comparatively (if still weakly) electron-richer, so the incoming electrophile is directed there.

</details>

## 4. Carbonium Ions

**Q4.1.** Rank the following carbocations by stability: methyl, primary (1°), secondary (2°), tertiary (3°), allylic.

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

$$\text{methyl} < 1° < 2° < 3° < \text{allylic}$$

Note: this ranking assumes a simple, non-resonance-stabilised allylic cation is being compared fairly against a simple tertiary cation — in practice a resonance-stabilised allylic or benzylic cation can rival or exceed tertiary stability, and exact placement depends on the specific substituents.

</details>

---

**Q4.2.** Explain hyperconjugation and why it makes tertiary carbocations more stable than primary ones.

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

Hyperconjugation is the partial overlap of a filled C–H (or C–C) σ bonding orbital on an adjacent carbon with the empty p orbital of the carbocation, delocalising electron density into the empty orbital and lowering the ion's energy. A tertiary carbocation has three adjacent alkyl groups, giving up to nine C–H σ bonds available for hyperconjugative donation, versus far fewer for a primary cation — more donating bonds means more stabilisation.

</details>

---

**Q4.3.** A secondary carbocation formed during an SN1 reaction is observed to rearrange to a tertiary carbocation before the nucleophile attacks. Name this process and explain the driving force.

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

This is a 1,2-hydride shift (or 1,2-methyl shift, depending on the substrate), a type of Wagner–Meerwein rearrangement. A hydrogen (or alkyl group) migrates with its bonding electron pair from the adjacent carbon to the electron-deficient cationic carbon. The driving force is thermodynamic: the resulting tertiary cation is significantly more stable than the secondary one due to greater hyperconjugative and inductive stabilisation, so the system rearranges before the (comparatively slow) nucleophilic attack step can occur.

</details>

---

**Q4.4.** Why is the vinyl cation (C=CH⁺) exceptionally unstable compared to even a primary alkyl cation?

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

The empty orbital on a vinyl cation is an sp hybrid orbital in the plane of the molecule, not a p orbital perpendicular to a π system, so it cannot be stabilised by hyperconjugation or resonance the way alkyl or allylic cations can. sp orbitals also hold electron density closer to the nucleus, making the empty sp orbital higher in energy and harder to stabilise inductively. Vinyl cations are so unstable that vinyl halides essentially never undergo SN1 solvolysis under normal conditions.

</details>

## 5. Carbanions

**Q5.1.** Rank by stability: CH₃⁻, C₆H₅⁻ (phenyl), CH₂=CH−CH₂⁻ (allyl), HC≡C⁻ (acetylide).

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

$$\text{CH}_3^- < \text{allyl} < \text{phenyl} < \text{acetylide}$$

Acetylide sits highest because the lone pair occupies an sp orbital with high s-character, holding the electrons closer to the nucleus and lowering their energy. Phenyl is also sp²-stabilised and somewhat resonance-assisted within the ring system. Allyl benefits from resonance delocalisation across two carbons. Methyl has none of these stabilising features.

</details>

---

**Q5.2.** Why does increasing s-character at the carbanion carbon increase its stability?

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

Orbitals with more s-character hold electron density, on average, closer to the positively charged nucleus (s orbitals are lower in energy and more penetrating than p orbitals). A carbanion lone pair sitting in an orbital with more s-character is therefore held more tightly and at lower energy, which is exactly what "more stable" means for a species carrying excess electron density. This is why acetylide (sp, 50% s-character) is far more stable than an alkyl carbanion (sp³, 25% s-character).

</details>

---

**Q5.3.** Explain, mechanistically, why a Grignard reagent (R–MgX) behaves as a carbanion equivalent even though the carbon–magnesium bond is not fully ionic.

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

The C–Mg bond is highly polarised because carbon is significantly more electronegative than magnesium, giving carbon a substantial partial negative charge (δ−). This makes the carbon strongly nucleophilic and basic in its reactivity — it attacks electrophilic centres like carbonyl carbons and readily deprotonates weak acids — even though X-ray and spectroscopic evidence show the bond retains significant covalent character rather than existing as a fully dissociated R⁻ and Mg²⁺X⁺ ion pair.

</details>

---

**Q5.4.** Terminal alkynes (pKₐ ≈ 25) can be deprotonated by sodium amide (NaNH₂, conjugate acid NH₃, pKₐ ≈ 38) but not by sodium hydroxide (conjugate acid H₂O, pKₐ ≈ 15.7). Justify this using pKₐ values and carbanion stability.

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

A base can only deprotonate an acid if the base's conjugate acid has a higher pKₐ than the acid being deprotonated — otherwise the equilibrium lies on the wrong side. NH₃ (pKₐ ≈ 38) is a far weaker acid than the terminal alkyne (pKₐ ≈ 25), so its conjugate base NH₂⁻ is more than strong enough to remove the alkyne proton and push the equilibrium essentially to completion. Water (pKₐ ≈ 15.7) is a stronger acid than the terminal alkyne, meaning OH⁻ is too weak a base — the reverse reaction (acetylide grabbing a proton back from water) is strongly favoured instead.

</details>

## 6. SN1 Reactions

**Q6.1.** Write the SN1 rate law and explain why nucleophile concentration does not appear in it.

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

$$\text{Rate} = k[\text{substrate}]$$

The mechanism has two steps: slow, rate-determining ionisation of the substrate to form a carbocation, followed by fast nucleophilic attack. Since the rate law only reflects the species involved up to and including the rate-determining step, and the nucleophile only participates in the fast second step, its concentration has no effect on the overall observed rate.

</details>

∙

**Q6.2.** A chiral secondary alkyl halide undergoes solvolysis in aqueous ethanol and gives a racemic product. Explain this outcome mechanistically.

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

Ionisation produces a planar, sp²-hybridised carbocation with the empty p orbital equally accessible from either face. The nucleophile (water/ethanol) can then attack from either the front face (retention) or the back face (inversion) with roughly equal probability, producing a near 1:1 mixture of both stereoisomers — i.e., a racemic (or nearly racemic) product, even though the starting material was a single enantiomer.

</details>

---

**Q6.3.** Rank the following substrates by expected SN1 reaction rate: tert-butyl bromide, isopropyl bromide, n-propyl bromide, methyl bromide.

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

$$\text{methyl} < n\text{-propyl (1°)} < \text{isopropyl (2°)} < tert\text{-butyl (3°)}$$

SN1 rate tracks carbocation stability directly, since ionisation is rate-determining. Tertiary substrates form the most stable carbocation and react fastest; methyl halides cannot form a viable carbocation at all and essentially don't undergo SN1.

</details>

---

**Q6.4.** Doubling the substrate concentration in an SN1 reaction doubles the rate, but doubling the nucleophile concentration has no effect. A kinetics student proposes this rules out any role for the nucleophile in the mechanism. Is this reasoning correct?

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

No. The nucleophile is essential to the mechanism — it's required in the second step to trap the carbocation and form product — but its concentration doesn't influence the rate because that step is fast and not rate-limiting. Kinetics reveals which species are involved up to the rate-determining step, not which species are mechanistically necessary overall. This distinction (kinetic role vs. mechanistic role) is a common conceptual trap.

</details>

## 7. SN2 Reactions

**Q7.1.** Explain Walden inversion using the "umbrella" analogy.

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

As the nucleophile attacks from the side directly opposite the leaving group, the three remaining substituents on the carbon flip through the transition state the way an umbrella turns inside-out in a strong wind. The spatial arrangement of the other three groups is inverted relative to the starting material, so a stereocentre with R configuration (for example) typically ends up with S configuration in the product — a single, clean inversion, not racemisation.

</details>

---

**Q7.2.** Why does SN2 rate decrease sharply going from methyl to primary to secondary to tertiary substrates, in contrast to SN1?

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

SN2 requires the nucleophile to approach and bond to the carbon from the backside in a single concerted step, so the transition state is sensitive to steric bulk around that carbon. Each additional alkyl substituent physically blocks the nucleophile's approach path, raising the energy of the transition state. Tertiary substrates are so crowded that backside attack is essentially impossible, which is the opposite trend from SN1, where more substituents stabilise the (sterically irrelevant) planar carbocation instead.

</details>

---

**Q7.3.** Predict the effect of switching from a polar protic solvent (e.g., ethanol) to a polar aprotic solvent (e.g., DMSO) on the rate of an SN2 reaction with a small anionic nucleophile like Cl⁻.

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

The rate increases, often substantially. In protic solvents, hydrogen bonding from the solvent surrounds and stabilises the anionic nucleophile, effectively "caging" it and lowering its nucleophilicity. Polar aprotic solvents can't hydrogen bond to the anion, leaving it much more "naked" and reactive, which lowers the activation energy for backside attack and speeds up the SN2 step considerably.

</details>

---

**Q7.4.** A reaction of (R)-2-bromobutane with NaI in acetone (the Finkelstein reaction) is monitored, and the product is found to be (S)-2-iodobutane. Confirm this is consistent with an SN2 mechanism.

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

Yes — this is exactly the expected outcome. Iodide attacks from the face opposite the departing bromide in a single concerted step, forcing inversion of configuration at the stereocentre. Even though the priority of iodine versus bromine in CIP rules can sometimes make an inversion look like a "retention" in the R/S label, in this specific case the spatial inversion does correspond to a clean R → S label change, confirming backside attack rather than any carbocation intermediate (which would have given a racemic mixture instead).

</details>

## 8. E1 Reactions

**Q8.1.** Explain, mechanistically, why E1 and SN1 reactions are always in competition with each other.

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

Both pathways share the identical first step: rate-determining ionisation of the substrate to form a carbocation intermediate. Once that carbocation exists, it faces a fork — a nucleophile can attack the electrophilic carbon directly (SN1 substitution product), or a base can remove a proton from an adjacent β-carbon, kicking electron density into a new π bond (E1 elimination product). Because the same intermediate can go either way, conditions that favour one pathway (like a strong base/poor nucleophile, or heat) shift the ratio toward elimination, and vice versa.

</details>

---

**Q8.2.** State Zaitsev's rule and explain the thermodynamic reasoning behind it.

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

Zaitsev's rule: the major product of an elimination reaction is generally the more substituted (and therefore more stable) alkene. More substituted alkenes are lower in energy because additional alkyl groups donate electron density into the π system through hyperconjugation, similar to how alkyl groups stabilise carbocations. Since E1 proceeds through a carbocation intermediate and the product-determining step (proton loss) is under thermodynamic-like control at the branch point, the more stable transition state leading to the more stable alkene is favoured.

</details>

---

**Q8.3.** A tertiary alkyl halide is heated in ethanol with no added base. Would you expect the major product to be from substitution or elimination? Explain.

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

Elimination (E1) is favoured as the major pathway under these conditions. Heat thermodynamically favours elimination because entropy increases (one molecule becomes two, or the transition state has more free-rotor character), and a tertiary substrate ionises readily to give a stable carbocation that a weak nucleophile like ethanol is poorly suited to trap efficiently compared to acting as a weak base toward a β-hydrogen. In practice, both SN1 and E1 products typically form as a mixture, but the elimination fraction rises substantially with temperature and substrate branching.

</details>

---

**Q8.4.** Why can a carbocation rearrangement in an E1 reaction lead to a product with a different carbon skeleton than the starting alkyl halide?

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

If the initially formed carbocation is not the most stable possible ion, a 1,2-hydride or 1,2-alkyl shift can occur before proton loss, converting it into a more stable carbocation positioned on a different carbon. Elimination then proceeds from this rearranged cation, meaning the double bond — and the resulting alkene's connectivity — reflects the rearranged skeleton rather than the position implied by simply removing the leaving group and a neighbouring β-hydrogen from the original structure.

</details>

## 9. E2 Reactions

**Q9.1.** Explain why the anti-periplanar geometry is required for E2 elimination, in terms of orbital overlap.

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

E2 is concerted: the base removes a β-hydrogen at the same time the leaving group departs and a new π bond forms between the α and β carbons. For the emerging p orbitals (from the breaking C–H and C–LG σ bonds) to align into a continuous, bonding π system, the C–H and C–LG bonds must be parallel to each other, which occurs only in the anti-periplanar (180°) arrangement. Any other dihedral angle leaves the developing p orbitals misaligned, preventing efficient overlap and raising the transition-state energy sharply.

</details>

---

**Q9.2.** Distinguish Zaitsev's rule from Hofmann's rule and state the condition under which Hofmann's rule dominates.

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

Zaitsev's rule favours the more substituted, thermodynamically more stable alkene and applies with small, unhindered bases. Hofmann's rule favours the less substituted alkene and dominates when a bulky base (such as tert-butoxide) is used — the base's steric bulk makes it much easier to reach a less hindered, more accessible β-hydrogen (typically on a terminal or less substituted carbon) than to reach a more crowded one, so the kinetically favoured product wins over the thermodynamically favoured one.

</details>

---

**Q9.3.** meso-2,3-Dibromobutane and (±)-2,3-dibromobutane give different alkene products (or different stereochemical outcomes) upon E2 elimination. Explain why stereospecificity produces this difference.

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

Because E2 requires strict anti-periplanar geometry, each diastereomer can only access specific eclipsing/staggered conformations that place particular H and Br groups anti to each other, and these differ between the meso and the (±) (d,l) diastereomers due to their different relative spatial arrangements of substituents. This forces meso-2,3-dibromobutane and (±)-2,3-dibromobutane to eliminate through geometrically distinct anti-periplanar arrangements, producing different alkene geometries (e.g., one giving predominantly the E-alkene, the other the Z-alkene) — direct experimental evidence that E2 is concerted and stereospecific rather than proceeding through a planar carbocation intermediate.

</details>

---

**Q9.4.** A secondary alkyl bromide is treated with a strong, non-bulky base like ethoxide (CH₃CH₂O⁻) in high concentration. Would you predict SN2 or E2 to dominate, and why?

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

This is genuinely competitive and depends on the specific substrate, but a strong, small, high-concentration base tends to favour E2 over SN2, especially as substrate branching increases toward secondary. Ethoxide is a strong base and a decent-but-sterically-modest nucleophile; high base concentration increases the rate of both SN2 and E2 (both are bimolecular, second-order processes), but E2's transition state has less steric demand at the reacting carbon than SN2's backside-attack transition state does, so with a secondary substrate elimination often becomes the major pathway, particularly at elevated temperature.

</details>

## 10. Addition Reactions

**Q10.1.** State Markovnikov's rule and explain its electronic basis for HBr addition to propene.

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

Markovnikov's rule: in the ionic addition of HX to an unsymmetrical alkene, the hydrogen preferentially bonds to the carbon that already has more hydrogens, and the halide ends up on the more substituted carbon. For propene, protonation occurs at the terminal (less substituted) carbon because this generates the more stable secondary carbocation, rather than protonating the internal carbon, which would generate a less stable primary carbocation. The more stable carbocation forms faster (lower activation energy), so it dominates the product distribution, and Br⁻ then attacks that secondary cation.

</details>

---

**Q10.2.** Explain the peroxide effect (Kharasch effect) and why it applies specifically to HBr and not to HCl or HI.

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

In the presence of peroxides, HBr addition switches from the ionic, Markovnikov-selective mechanism to a radical chain mechanism, which delivers the anti-Markovnikov product because the regiochemistry-determining step becomes addition of a bromine radical to the alkene (favouring the more stable, more substituted radical) rather than protonation. This effect is essentially unique to HBr because the bond dissociation energies and propagation-step thermodynamics happen to make the radical chain both initiable and efficiently propagating for bromine; for HCl the radical chain is thermodynamically unfavourable at the H-abstraction step, and for HI the C–I bond formed is too weak relative to the H–I bond broken, making the alternative chain endothermic and non-viable.

</details>

---

**Q10.3.** Why does catalytic hydrogenation of an alkene proceed with syn addition of hydrogen, while bromination with Br₂ proceeds with anti addition?

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

Catalytic hydrogenation occurs on the surface of a heterogeneous metal catalyst — the alkene adsorbs onto the metal surface, and both hydrogen atoms are delivered from that same surface to the same face of the double bond, necessarily giving syn addition. Bromination, by contrast, proceeds through a cyclic bromonium ion intermediate: after the first bromine bridges both carbons from one face, the second bromide ion must attack from the opposite face (backside, SN2-like) to open the strained three-membered ring, which forces anti addition.

</details>

---

**Q10.4.** Grignard reagents add to carbonyl compounds via nucleophilic addition. Explain why this addition is nucleophilic (rather than electrophilic) in terms of which species initiates the attack.

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

In nucleophilic addition, the attacking species is electron-rich and seeks out an electron-poor centre — here, the carbanion-like carbon of the Grignard reagent (δ− due to the polarised C–Mg bond) attacks the electrophilic carbonyl carbon (δ+ due to oxygen's electronegativity pulling electron density away). This is the reverse polarity pattern from electrophilic addition to alkenes, where the electron-rich π bond is attacked by an electron-poor species; the carbonyl's inherent polarisation (C is δ+, unlike the non-polarised alkene π bond) is what makes nucleophilic, rather than electrophilic, addition the operative pathway.

</details>

## 11. Cross-Topic Challenge Questions

**Q11.1.** A tertiary alkyl bromide is treated separately with (a) a weak nucleophile in a polar protic solvent with no added base, and (b) a strong, bulky base like potassium tert-butoxide. Predict the dominant mechanism and major product type in each case.

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

(a) SN1/E1 mixture, since the substrate ionises readily and the weak nucleophile/base can't force a concerted bimolecular pathway — expect a mix of substitution and Zaitsev-favoured elimination products, both proceeding through the same carbocation intermediate.

(b) E2 dominates almost exclusively. Tertiary substrates are far too hindered for SN2, and a strong bulky base like tert-butoxide is a poor nucleophile but an excellent base for a concerted, anti-periplanar E2 elimination, with the bulk additionally favouring the Hofmann (less substituted) alkene where a choice of β-hydrogens exists.

</details>

---

**Q11.2.** Explain how the concept of "stability" connects a carbocation intermediate (Topic 4) to the regiochemistry of both SN1 substitution (Topic 6) and Markovnikov addition (Topic 10).

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

All three phenomena are governed by the same underlying principle: whichever pathway generates the more stable carbocation is kinetically favoured, because a more stable transition state leading toward that cation has a lower activation energy (Hammond postulate — for an endothermic ionisation step, the transition state resembles the product cation). In SN1, this determines which substrate reacts fastest; in Markovnikov addition, this determines which carbon gets protonated first. The stability order (3° > 2° > 1°, with resonance-stabilised cations often exceeding all of these) is the common thread linking carbocation structure directly to observed reaction outcomes across otherwise distinct reaction types.

</details>

---

**Q11.3.** A chemist proposes that increasing solvent polarity will speed up an SN2 reaction between a neutral nucleophile (like NH₃) and a neutral substrate, but slow down an SN2 reaction between an anionic nucleophile (like OH⁻) and a neutral substrate. Justify or refute this using charge-distribution reasoning.

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

This is correct, and it follows from how the transition state's charge distribution compares to the starting materials'. With a neutral nucleophile attacking a neutral substrate, the SN2 transition state develops partial positive and negative charge that wasn't present in the reactants, so a more polar solvent stabilises that developing charge and speeds up the reaction. With an anionic nucleophile, the negative charge is already concentrated on the small nucleophile in the reactant state and becomes more diffuse (spread over a larger transition-state structure) as the reaction proceeds — a polar solvent stabilises the concentrated reactant-state charge more effectively than the diffuse transition-state charge, raising the effective activation energy and slowing the reaction.

</details>

---

**Q11.4.** A secondary alkyl chloride is dissolved in a solvent mixture and gives predominantly inversion of configuration with fast kinetics that depend on nucleophile concentration. A structurally similar tertiary alkyl chloride under the same conditions gives predominantly racemisation with kinetics independent of nucleophile concentration. Identify the mechanism operating in each case and explain what structural feature causes the switch.

<details>

The secondary substrate is reacting via SN2 (inversion, rate depends on nucleophile concentration — consistent with a concerted bimolecular mechanism), while the tertiary substrate is reacting via SN1 (racemisation, rate independent of nucleophile concentration — consistent with a planar carbocation intermediate attacked from either face). The switch is driven by sterics and cation stability together: the tertiary centre is too hindered for backside attack (ruling out SN2) but forms a carbocation stable enough to make ionisation kinetically accessible (enabling SN1), while the secondary centre is open enough for backside attack and doesn't form a cation stable enough to make ionisation competitive.
