# Picric Acid (2,4,6-Trinitrophenol) — Identification

## Physical Properties

* Bright yellow crystalline solid with a bitter taste.
* M.P. ≈ 122–123 °C.
* Stains skin, wool, and silk yellow.
* An unusually strong acid for a phenol (comparable to mineral acids), because three electron-withdrawing –NO₂ groups strongly stabilise the picrate anion.
* **Safety note:** dry picric acid and its metal salts (especially picrates) are shock- and heat-sensitive explosives. In a teaching lab, always handle it wetted and in small quantities; never let it dry out inside metal caps/fittings.

## Tests

### (i) Litmus / NaHCO₃ Test

Picric acid solution + blue litmus paper → turns red.
Picric acid + NaHCO₃ → effervescence of CO₂.

**Observation:** Strongly acidic reaction, unusual for a phenol.

**Inference:** The three –NO₂ groups make the phenolic O–H strongly ionisable, so picric acid behaves like a strong acid, unlike ordinary phenol.

### (ii) Neutral FeCl₃ Test

Picric acid + neutral FeCl₃
→ **no violet colour**

**Observation:** No characteristic phenol colour appears.

**Inference:** Distinguishes picric acid from ordinary phenols — all ring positions are occupied/deactivated, so the classic Fe³⁺–phenoxide colour complex does not form the same way.

### (iii) Reaction with Alkali

Picric acid + NaOH/KOH solution
→ dissolves readily, deep yellow solution (sodium/potassium picrate)

**Observation:** Deep yellow solution forms as the salt.

### (iv) Reaction with KCN

Picric acid + KCN solution, warm gently
→ deep red colour (isopurpurate)

**Observation:** Red colouration confirms the picrate structure.

## Comparison Note

Picric acid is the only compound in the acids group that turns litmus red and effervesces with NaHCO₃ *without* being a classic carboxylic acid — and the only phenol-type compound that fails the FeCl₃ violet test. See [16_phenol.md](16_phenol.md) for the contrast.
