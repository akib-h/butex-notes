# Viva Q&A — Identification of Organic Compounds

Practical viva questions across all four functional-group families (alcohols, carboxylic acids, carbonyl compounds, miscellaneous). Each answer is collapsed — click to reveal.

<details>
<summary>Why does methanol fail the iodoform test while ethanol passes?</summary>
Iodoform test needs a CH₃CH(OH)–R or CH₃CO–R fragment. Methanol has only one carbon (CH₃OH), so there's no such fragment; ethanol (CH₃CH₂OH) has the required CH₃CH(OH)– grouping after oxidation to CH₃CHO/CH₃CO–.
</details>

<details>
<summary>Why does tert-butyl alcohol resist oxidation by acidified K₂Cr₂O₇?</summary>
Oxidation of an alcohol by chromic acid requires an α-hydrogen on the carbon bearing –OH. In tert-butyl alcohol, that carbon is bonded to three methyl groups and no hydrogen, so it cannot be oxidised to a carbonyl compound under normal conditions.
</details>

<details>
<summary>Why must the Lucas test rely on turbidity timing rather than colour change?</summary>
The Lucas reagent (ZnCl₂/conc. HCl) converts the alcohol to an insoluble alkyl chloride via a carbocation intermediate; the reaction is an SN1 process, so its rate — and hence how fast the oily/cloudy alkyl chloride layer appears — depends directly on carbocation stability: 3° (instant) > 2° (minutes) > 1° (no reaction at room temperature).
</details>

<details>
<summary>What distinguishes formic acid from all other carboxylic acids in this syllabus?</summary>
Formic acid (HCOOH) structurally contains an aldehyde-like –CHO unit fused to the –COOH, so unlike acetic, oxalic, benzoic, salicylic, and picric acid, it reduces Tollens' reagent (silver mirror) and Fehling's solution (red Cu₂O ppt.) — behaving as both an acid and a reducing aldehyde.
</details>

<details>
<summary>Why is picric acid a much stronger acid than ordinary phenol?</summary>
The three electron-withdrawing –NO₂ groups (at 2, 4, 6 positions) strongly stabilise the picrate (phenoxide) anion by resonance and induction, making the O–H far more ionisable — strong enough to liberate CO₂ from NaHCO₃, unlike normal phenol.
</details>

<details>
<summary>Why does benzaldehyde give a weak/negative Fehling's test but a positive Tollens' test?</summary>
Fehling's reagent is a milder oxidant that works best on aliphatic aldehydes; aromatic aldehydes like benzaldehyde react poorly or not at all with Fehling's, but Tollens' (a stronger, more general oxidant for –CHO) still oxidises it, giving a silver mirror.
</details>

<details>
<summary>Why does acetone give a positive iodoform test but a negative Tollens'/Fehling's test?</summary>
Acetone has the CH₃CO– grouping needed for iodoform, but as a ketone it has no H on the carbonyl carbon, so it cannot be oxidised further — it is not a reducing agent toward Tollens'/Fehling's the way an aldehyde is.
</details>

<details>
<summary>Why does chlorobenzene not give a precipitate with AgNO₃ directly, unlike an alkyl chloride?</summary>
The C–Cl bond in chlorobenzene has partial double-bond character due to conjugation of the chlorine lone pair with the aromatic ring, making it far less reactive toward nucleophilic substitution; the chlorine is essentially covalent and non-ionisable under these mild conditions.
</details>

<details>
<summary>Why does nitrobenzene need to be reduced before it can be identified by the carbylamine test?</summary>
The carbylamine test only detects primary amines (–NH₂). Nitrobenzene's nitrogen is in the form of –NO₂, not –NH₂, so it gives no direct carbylamine reaction. Reducing it (Sn/HCl or Fe/HCl) converts –NO₂ to –NH₂ (aniline), which then tests positive.
</details>

<details>
<summary>Why does urea give a violet colour in the biuret test, but not before heating?</summary>
Fresh urea itself doesn't have the biuret linkage. Heating two urea molecules together (with loss of NH₃) first forms biuret (H₂N-CO-NH-CO-NH₂), which then chelates Cu²⁺ ions in alkaline solution to give the characteristic violet-pink complex.
</details>

<details>
<summary>How do you distinguish acetamide from acetanilide using NaOH?</summary>
Acetamide releases free ammonia readily on warming with NaOH (litmus test positive quickly), because the nitrogen is unsubstituted. Acetanilide's nitrogen is substituted by a phenyl group, so simple base treatment is slower/weaker; full hydrolysis is needed to liberate aniline and acetate, and aniline is then confirmed separately (carbylamine test).
</details>

## Quick decision path for an unknown sample

1. **State/smell first** — narrows the family before any wet test.
2. **NaHCO₃ effervescence?** → carboxylic acid (or, unusually, picric acid). No effervescence but violet with FeCl₃ → phenol.
3. **2,4-DNP positive?** → carbonyl compound present.
4. **Tollens'/Fehling's** → aldehyde vs ketone (strong Fehling = aliphatic aldehyde; Tollens-only/weak Fehling = aromatic aldehyde).
5. **Iodoform test** → confirms a CH₃CO–/CH₃CH(OH)– unit (ethanol, isopropanol, acetaldehyde, acetone, acetophenone positive; methanol, tert-butanol, benzaldehyde negative).
6. **Carbylamine test (foul smell)** → primary amine.
7. **NaOH + heat → NH₃** → simple amide (urea, benzamide, acetamide); acetanilide needs full hydrolysis first.
8. **Beilstein green flame + AgNO₃ negative directly** → aryl halide.

## Common lab mistakes to call out in viva

- Adding NaOH too fast in the iodoform test before the faint I₂ colour persists — masks the CHI₃ ppt.
- Using a greasy test tube for Tollens'/Fehling's — kills the silver-mirror/Cu₂O result.
- Testing FeCl₃ on an unneutralised acidic solution — masks the phenolic colour.
- Skipping the Beilstein wire pre-clean (heat to no colour) — gives false-positive green flames.
- Not cooling the diazotisation mixture to 0–5 °C — decomposes the diazonium salt, false negative on azo coupling.
